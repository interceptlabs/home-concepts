Concept D · explore pages · for review

Rethinking the quiet pages.

Three of the explore pages — What we solve, InterceptOS, and the agent roster — have accumulated interaction machinery the content doesn’t need: picker grids, steppers, prev/next, modals-behind-tabs. The live site’s original sections were simpler and more confident: one surface, everything visible, one deliberate choice at most.

Below are three directions per page, each built as a working mock with the real copy. They’re labeled (P‑A, OS‑B, R‑C…) so you can call out winners, mixes, or kills per section.

One through-line worth considering

The four problems are the story of all three pages. The live site already implies it: What we solve names the four problems → InterceptOS shows the production line for each one → the agents are the people on that line. If each page keys off the same four problems — same names, same order, same accent — the three pages stop being three separate widgets and become chapters: the problem → the system → the crew. Concepts P‑B, OS‑A, and R‑C form that trilogy.

Section 1 · What we solve

We love a chewy problem.

What the original did

Four plain tabs, one panel: quote, attribution, all three tells, the stat, and the bridge — everything visible at once, crossfading between problems. One choice, then you just read.

Where the current page drifted

The problem names now live in four boxed picker cards and repeat inside the panel. The tells hide behind a “More +” disclosure. The bridge floats at the panel bottom, detached from the quote it answers. Two dark surfaces compete for the same job.

The story this page needs to tell: “Your peers are saying this out loud → the data agrees → here’s how we attack it.” Quote, proof, answer — in that order, no hiding.

P-A

The Reading Room

No selection state at all — the four problems become one continuous read, like a well-set editorial feature. A quiet index on the left tracks where you are and jumps on click; each problem runs quote → attribution → tells → stat → bridge in a single uninterrupted column. The page trusts the reader to scroll.

Trade-off: strongest for reading all four; weakest if a visitor only cares about one and wants instant comparison. Scroll depth replaces clicks.

P-B

Problem / Answer

The original live-site model, restaged for a standalone page. Four typographic tabs — no boxes — and below them one composition: the quote and tells on the left (the problem, in the buyer’s words), the stat and bridge on the right in the deep blue (the evidence and our answer). Choose a problem; everything else is just reading. The layout itself performs the story: problem on one side, answer on the other.

Trade-off: one interaction to learn (the tabs), and the panel must fit the longest problem’s content. Closest to the original’s spirit.

P-C

Four on the Wall

All four problems visible simultaneously — a 2×2 wall of quote cards, each carrying its stat. You see the whole landscape in one glance and recognize your own problem before touching anything. Selecting a card turns it ink-dark and opens the tells and bridge beneath the wall. Scanning first, depth on commit.

Trade-off: best first-glance comprehension of “there are four, which is mine?”; the detail area sits below the fold-line of the wall, so the quote→answer pairing is less immediate than P‑B.

Section 2 · InterceptOS

The AI-native layer behind better work.

What the original did

Pick a problem, and the whole four-stage line renders at once — Diagnostic → Core → Innovation → Outcome — with connectors between stages and agent chips inside each. A pipeline you could see end-to-end.

Where the current page drifted

The pipeline became a stepper: one stage visible at a time, Prev/Next buttons, a rail of chips to track where you are. The one thing this section must communicate — there is a system, and it runs end-to-end to an outcome — is exactly what a stepper hides.

The story this page needs to tell: “Your problem goes in one end; a finished outcome comes out the other. Here is the line, and the agents working it.” Show the whole line.

OS-A

The Assembly Line

The original pipeline, brought back and given room. All four stages sit in one horizontal line with connectors; the Outcome stage is staged differently — deep blue, Flarepop headline — because it’s the payoff the other three exist for. Problem selection is four quiet text tabs. Nothing is hidden, nothing steps.

Trade-off: four columns means tighter copy measure per stage; on narrow screens the line folds vertical (which is OS‑B anyway).

OS-B

The Rail

The same full pipeline turned vertical — a project plan you read downward. Stage tags hold a strict left column; names, descriptions, and agent chips get a full comfortable measure on the right. The Outcome lands at the bottom in a Flarepop wash, like a delivered result. This is the most readable version of the system — alignment does all the structural work, no connectors needed.

Trade-off: taller than one viewport with all four stages; less of a “diagram” feeling than OS‑A. Naturally perfect on mobile.

OS-C

Input → OS → Output

The most conceptual take: InterceptOS drawn as an actual operating system — a black chassis in the middle with the three working stages compacted inside it, the selected problem entering from the left, the outcome leaving on the right in blue. One diagram makes the whole claim: this is a machine; problems become outcomes. Best matched to the page’s “AI-native layer” framing.

Trade-off: stage descriptions compress to fit the chassis (full text could reveal on hover or below); the strongest visual metaphor of the three but the least room for copy.

Section 3 · The agent roster

Meet the capabilities inside InterceptOS.

What the original did

Category tabs over a card grid, with a compact detail on click. Reasonable on a homepage where the roster is one section among ten — the grid was a teaser, not a reference.

Where the current page drifted

A dedicated roster page kept the homepage’s gating: four tabs → four visible cards → modal. Thirteen agents, and you can never see more than four names at once. On a page whose entire job is “here’s the bench,” the bench is hidden.

The story this page needs to tell: “This is the whole crew — named, specialized, and already proven on work like yours. Find the ones your problem needs.” Show all thirteen.

R-A

The Full Roster

One scannable index of all thirteen — name, specialty, one-line role, category — like a masthead or a team sheet. Filters thin the list instead of paginating it; a row opens inline for the full description, the problems it solves, and the sample work. No modal, no tabs, nothing off-screen. The utilitarian honesty of a roster is the design.

Trade-off: the least “showcase” of the three — typography carries everything. (Row-open below is live; try Watchtower.)

R-B

The Bench

Every agent on the wall at once — a dense card wall grouped under the four category headers, so the categories organize instead of gate. Cards stay small (name, specialty, role line); hover flips a card to ink; click opens the approved case-modal-style detail. You get the full-bench impression in one screen and depth stays one click away.

Trade-off: keeps the modal as the detail surface (already built and approved); cross-category agents live under their primary group with the overlap noted in the detail.

R-C

Sorted by Problem

Flip the axis: organize the roster by the four problems instead of by discipline. Each problem band lists the agents that actually work it — some appear twice, which is the point: the roster stops being an org chart and becomes an answer to “who’s on my problem?”. This is the concept that closes the trilogy — the same four problems that opened What we solve staff themselves here.

Trade-off: agents repeat across bands (deliberate but unusual); discipline view disappears unless offered as a secondary sort.

Where I’d land

As a trilogy: P‑B + OS‑A + R‑C. All three key off the same four problems, all three show everything on one surface, and each page gets exactly one interaction (choose your problem) instead of nested machinery. It’s also the closest return to what the live site already does well.

Safest single swaps: P‑B and OS‑A are direct upgrades with no downside I can see. For the roster, R‑A is the conservative pick if R‑C’s repeated agents feel odd — or R‑B if you want to keep the new modal doing the detail work.

Call out winners by label and I’ll build them into the explore pages.
